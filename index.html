Why these behave differently

  • Pixel size is a hard constraint. File size is a window. SSC’s signature is 140 × 60 px and between 10 KB and 20 KB. A tool that only caps the maximum will happily produce 6 KB for a 10 KB floor and print “Success!”. This one refuses, with a reason.
  • It verifies its own output. The finished file’s dimensions are re-read from its own JPEG header before you are offered the download, rather than trusting the tool’s own report.
  • Nothing leaves your device. The tools make no network requests at all once loaded. They can be copied to a USB stick and opened with no internet connection.

Where the numbers come from

Secondary websites contradict each other on these requirements — some published 315 × 157 px for a signature the official SSC document states as 140 × 60 px, others quote a 20 KB minimum for an MPSC photograph whose official instructions state a 50 KB maximum and no minimum at all, and others still give a UPSC signature as 140 × 60 px when the official instructions ask for something else entirely. So every preset carries its own provenance, and says plainly whether a primary source was read.

A suspicion that turned out to be wrong. Four IBPS presets were previously labelled “identical to the SSC spec, which suggests the note copied the SSC template”. IBPS publishes its own guideline, and it states exactly the same four numbers — 200 × 230 px / 20–50 KB for the photo, 140 × 60 px / 10–20 KB for the signature, 240 × 240 px for the thumb impression, 800 × 400 px / 50–100 KB for the declaration. It is the same template on purpose. Learn one set of numbers and it covers both exams. What the notes missed is that IBPS also requires a live photograph in addition to the scanned one.

One finding worth knowing: the risk runs the other way. The official SSC signature box is 140 × 60 px with a 10–20 KB window. Measured across 28 realistic signature scans, only 7 could reach the 10 KB floor at all. Telling someone to “shrink the signature” is exactly the wrong advice there — so that preset carries a caution and offers the larger documented box (472 × 157) instead.

Another: RRB asks for two things that cannot both be true. CEN No. 09/2025 wants a signature of 30–49 KB at a minimum of 140 × 60 px. At that size the file would need 3.66 bytes per pixel — more than JPEG can produce, and nearly three times what the same box actually reaches. Measured at every scan resolution the CEN permits, the smallest one that works is 400 DPI on the CEN's own 35 × 20 mm scan box, which is what the preset uses.

And UPSC's own two documents disagree with each other. The “Instructions for Uploading the Photo & Signature” cap the photograph at 200 KB and the signature at 100 KB, in 350–500 px. The OTR FAQ allows 300 KB for both and 1000 px. Where they conflict, the presets ship the intersection, so a file this tool produces cannot be refused for size by either document. The UPSC signature is also unlike any other here: it is the same signature written three times, stacked vertically, scanned as one image.

What these tools do not claim

They confirm that a file meets the stated requirements. They do not claim the portal will accept it — that depends on the live portal, and only you can confirm it. Always check on the official upload page before you rely on it.

Some requirements cannot be met by uploading a file at all. Where a portal demands a live photograph captured on its own site or app, the tool explains the rule and disables the upload rather than producing a file you cannot use.

HEIC is not supported. iPhone photos are usually HEIC by default and no browser other than Safari can decode it — convert to JPEG first. The tool says so rather than failing silently.
